The Absolute Basics

Studied from Desai, How Finance Works · written from scratch

1. The Hook: Two Words, One Subtraction

This module assumes nothing at all. It exists because two words — asset and liability — do almost all the work in every module that follows, and because the single most common question in personal finance, “how much money do I have,” turns out to be a trick question.

There are two sections here. The first asks what money functionally is, and answers it with one subtraction. The second asks what interest is, and answers it with one sentence that also happens to define what a bank does — which is why Module 03 can be about banking without introducing anything new.

2. Definitions

0.1 What Even Is Money, Functionally?

Start with a picture, not a definition. Imagine emptying your entire life onto a table: your bank card, your laptop, your bike, the phone in your hand, and next to it a stack of every bill and loan statement you owe. The table now has two piles. One pile is made of things that would put cash into your hand if you sold or spent them. The other pile is made of promises that will take cash out of your hand later. Every idea in this entire curriculum — balance sheets, credit, valuation, the lot — is just a more careful way of sorting those two piles and doing arithmetic on them.

The first pile has a name.

Definition 0.1 — Asset

Anything you own that has value.

An asset is anything on that table that points cash toward you: the money itself, yes, but also the laptop you could sell, the shares you could cash out, the security deposit you'll get back. It does not have to be cash today; it only has to be convertible to value at some point. The second pile is its mirror image.

Definition 0.2 — Liability

Anything you owe.

A liability points cash away from you: a credit-card balance, a student loan, the rent you've agreed to pay. Notice the symmetry — every liability you have is, from the other side of the table, somebody else's asset. Your student loan is an asset on the bank's books. That single fact is the seed of Module 03. Now the only question left is what happens when you set one pile against the other.

Definition 0.3 — Net Worth

What you actually have, once debts are subtracted.

$$\text{Net Worth} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}$$

Before the symbols, the mechanics. This is not really a “formula” in the sense of something you compute and forget — it is a definition of truth-telling. The left pile is a positive number. The right pile is a negative one. Net worth is what survives when they collide. Read the equation dynamically: anything that grows the asset pile (earning, saving, an investment rising) pushes net worth up dollar-for-dollar; anything that grows the liability pile (borrowing, a balance compounding) pushes it down the same way. Two people can earn identical salaries and have opposite net worths, entirely because of which pile each has been feeding.

Why This Matters

“How much money do I have” is a trick question if you only look at your bank balance, because the balance shows you one entry from the asset pile and none of the liability pile. A \$3,000 chequing balance sitting on top of a \$9,000 card balance is not \$3,000 of anything. Net worth is the single number that refuses to be fooled by a healthy bank balance — it is the score, and everything else is a move that changes the score.

0.2 What Even Is Interest?

Money, like an apartment, can be rented. If you let someone live in your apartment for a year, you expect rent; if you let someone use your money for a year, you expect interest. That is the whole idea — interest is rent on money — and the analogy is exact enough to carry you a surprisingly long way.

Definition 0.4 — Interest

The price of using someone else's money.

Read it from both ends of the lease. When your savings sit in a bank, you are the landlord: the bank is renting your money and pays you interest for it. When you carry a balance on a card, the roles flip: now you are the tenant, renting the lender's money, and you pay the rent. Same word, same arithmetic, opposite direction — and which direction you're facing is most of what separates building wealth from leaking it.

3. Worked Examples

From 0.1 — Net worth, computed step by step

Worked Example

You have \$2,000 in chequing, a laptop worth \$1,500, and a \$500 credit-card balance. Work it in three deliberate steps.

Step 1 — total the asset pile. Two things point cash toward you: the cash itself and the laptop you could sell. $2{,}000 + 1{,}500 = \$3{,}500$.

Step 2 — total the liability pile. One thing points cash away: the card balance. That's \$500.

Step 3 — subtract.

$$\text{Net Worth} = \underbrace{3{,}500}_{\text{assets}} - \underbrace{500}_{\text{liabilities}} = \$3{,}000$$

The chequing balance alone said \$2,000. The honest figure is \$3,000, because the laptop counted and the card debt was subtracted rather than ignored. The bank balance was neither.

From 0.2 — Interest, computed

Worked Example

\$1,000 sits in a savings account paying 4% per year. The 4% is the rent rate; the rent itself is that rate applied to the amount rented.

$$\text{Interest} = 1{,}000 \times 0.04 = \$40 \text{ in year one}$$

The bank pays you \$40 for the use of your \$1,000 — because the bank turns around and rents that money out to someone else for more. Paying depositors to borrow, then lending at a higher rate, is not a feature of a bank; it is the definition of one (Module 3).

That second example is worth sitting with, because it runs in both directions. When the bank pays you 4%, you are the lender collecting \$40. When you carry a \$1,000 balance at 20%, you are the borrower and the identical arithmetic — $1{,}000 \times 0.20 = \$200$ — is now pointed at you. Same machine, reversed polarity, which is exactly what Module 3's worked example makes concrete.

6. Interview Translation

The point of this section, here and in every module, is to convert what you just learned into the words you would actually say across a desk from a portfolio manager or a corporate-finance interviewer. They rarely ask for a definition; they ask you to connect the small idea to the big one.

Interview Translation

If asked “what is net worth, really?” don't recite the subtraction — locate it. Say:

“Net worth is the personal version of shareholders' equity on a company balance sheet: it's assets minus liabilities, the residual claim left over once everything owed is settled. A bank balance is a single asset line; net worth is the bottom line. That's why I'd never judge financial health from cash on hand — I'd judge it from the residual, and from whether it's trending up or down over time.”

That answer does three things an interviewer is listening for: it maps a personal term to the corporate one (net worth ↔ equity), it names the concept precisely (a residual claim), and it distinguishes a stock (net worth, measured at an instant) from a trajectory (the direction it's moving) — the exact snapshot-versus-video distinction Module 02 is built on.
