1. The Hook: One Formula, Rearranged Forever

Everything after this module — the amortization schedule in Module 03, the net present value in Module 04, the discounted cash flow in Module 7.3 — is one formula, rearranged. This is that formula.

Two ideas carry it. The first is that a dollar has a date attached to it, and that moving the date changes what it is worth. The second is that interest paid on interest already earned compounds, so the change is not linear. The module opens with a refresher on the two background facts it leans on, then builds both ideas from nothing.

3. Definitions

1.1 The Time Value of Money

Here is the analogy to hold onto before any symbols appear. A dollar is like a seed. A seed you plant today has all season to grow; the same seed planted in autumn does not. The seed hasn't changed — what changed is how much time it was given to do its one job. Money works the same way: a dollar in your hand today can be put to work immediately, so it is worth more than a dollar that only shows up next year. The dollar didn't change; the growing season did.

Definition 1.1 — TVM

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar later, because today's dollar can be invested immediately.

Because a dollar's worth depends on when it lands, we need a way to carry a dollar across time — forward to see what it becomes, or backward to see what a future dollar is worth right now. Those two directions have names.

Definition 1.2 & 1.3 — Present Value (PV) / Future Value (FV)

The source names these two together, because neither is meaningful alone: each is the other, moved across $n$ years at rate $r$. The formula below is the move, written in both directions.

$$FV = PV \times (1+r)^n \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^n}$$

Deconstructing the formula, before trusting the symbols

Do not memorise $FV = PV \times (1+r)^n$ as a shape. Build it. Ask what one year of growth does to a pile of money, then let the years stack up.

One year. Put down $PV$ dollars at rate $r$. At year's end you still have your original pile and the interest it earned: the pile ($PV$) plus its growth ($PV \times r$). Factor out the $PV$ and that is $PV \times (1 + r)$. So the entire engine is one move — multiply by $(1+r)$ — where the $1$ is “keep what you had” and the $r$ is “add this year's rent.”

Two years. Year two does the exact same move, but to year one's ending pile, not the original. So you multiply by $(1+r)$ a second time: $PV \times (1+r) \times (1+r) = PV \times (1+r)^2$. That is the whole secret of the exponent — $n$ is simply how many times in a row you apply the one-year move. Three years is three multiplications, ten years is ten. The power isn't decoration; it is a count of growing seasons.

Why it curves. Because each year multiplies the result of the last one, the growth is not a straight line — it bends upward, gently at first and then steeply, the way a rumour spreads to a growing audience. Turn the same machine around and the division form, $PV = FV / (1+r)^n$, does the reverse: it strips out the growing seasons to ask “what seed would have produced this harvest?” A larger $r$ or a longer $n$ means more seasons stripped away, so a future dollar shrinks harder the further off, or the more heavily discounted, it is. Sit with that sentence — it is the entire reason Module 04's NPV is so touchy about its discount rate.

1.2 Compound Interest

Compounding is what you just saw, named. The reason two years wasn't simply “twice one year” is that in year two the interest itself started earning interest. Picture a snowball rolling downhill: it doesn't just pick up snow, it picks up snow along a surface that keeps getting bigger, so each turn adds more than the last. Money left to compound is a snowball made of dollars.

Definition 1.4 — Compound Interest

Interest earned on previously earned interest.

4. Worked Examples

From 1.1 — Moving a dollar in both directions

Worked Example

Take \$1,000, a rate of 5% per year, and a term of 3 years. Apply the one-year move three times, watching each multiplication land on the previous result.

Year 1: $1{,}000 \times 1.05 = \$1{,}050.00$  (you kept your \$1,000 and added \$50 of rent).

Year 2: $1{,}050 \times 1.05 = \$1{,}102.50$  (this year's \$52.50 of interest is larger than last year's \$50 — the extra \$2.50 is interest earned on last year's interest).

Year 3: $1{,}102.50 \times 1.05 = \$1{,}157.63$.

Three multiplications in a row is exactly what the exponent packages up:

$$FV = 1{,}000 \times 1.05^3 = \$1{,}157.63$$

Now run the machine backward. If someone promises you \$1,157.63 in three years and money grows at 5%, what is that promise worth today? Undo the three multiplications by dividing: $1{,}157.63 / 1.05^3 = \$1{,}000$. The future sum is worth exactly the present sum — they are the same dollar, photographed at two different times.

5. Common Confusions

Common Confusion

A higher discount rate shrinks PV fast because of the exponent — always sanity-check the rate assumption.

This one is worth feeling rather than reading. In the sandbox, hold the principal and term fixed and drag the rate: the future value does not rise along a line, it bends. The same bend, pointed the other way, is what makes Module 4's NPV so sensitive to its discount-rate assumption.

8. Interview Translation

Time value is the single most-tested idea in an entry-level finance interview, because every valuation question is secretly a time-value question. You will not be asked to plug numbers — you'll be asked to explain why the machine behaves the way it does.

Interview Translation

If asked “why is a dollar today worth more than a dollar next year?” resist the textbook sentence and give the mechanism:

“Because today's dollar can be put to work immediately, so it compounds one extra period. Formally, future value is present value times $(1+r)$ raised to the number of periods — each period you multiply by $(1+r)$, once to keep the principal and once more to add that period's return. Discounting is just that machine in reverse: I divide by $(1+r)^n$ to ask what a future cash flow is worth now. It's the same operation behind bond pricing, NPV and a DCF — only the cash flows change.”

The follow-up you should expect: “What happens to present value if I raise the discount rate?” The crisp answer is it falls, and it falls faster for cash flows further out, because the rate compounds inside an exponent — a small change in $r$ is magnified by $n$. Being able to say why it falls non-linearly, rather than just that it falls, is the difference between having memorised a formula and having understood it. That same sensitivity is the risk you'll manage directly in Module 04 and Module 7.3.
