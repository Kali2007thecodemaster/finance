1. The Hook: A Snapshot and a Video

Module 00 gave you one number: net worth, assets minus liabilities. This module gives you the two documents that number lives in, and a second number that behaves completely differently.

The distinction between them is the whole module, and it is a distinction about time: one document describes an instant, the other describes an interval. Companies keep both for exactly this reason, and the reason applies unchanged to a person.

2. What You Already Half-Remember

What You Already Half-Remember — BUS 100

A balance sheet is a snapshot (owns vs. owes, one day); an income statement is a video (money in vs. out, over a period).

Hold on to the snapshot-versus-video framing — it is what makes the closing line of this module ("watch cash flow month-to-month, net worth year-to-year") follow rather than just sound sensible.

3. Definitions

2.1 Personal Balance Sheet

A balance sheet lists the two piles from Module 00 — assets on one side, liabilities on the other — frozen on a single day. But not every asset is equally spendable, and the word for “how quickly can I turn this into cash without taking a loss” is liquidity. Think of a spectrum of temperature. Cash is liquid — it pours. A term deposit is slushy; you can get it, but with a small delay or penalty. A car or a condo is frozen solid: real value, but you can't spend a corner of it this afternoon, and forcing a fast sale usually means selling cheap.

Definition 2.1 — Liquid Asset

Convertible to cash quickly without losing value. Contrast illiquid asset.

Liquidity is not the same as worth. A \$400,000 house is worth far more than \$400 in your pocket, yet the \$400 is what pays for groceries tonight. This is why net worth alone can mislead in the other direction too: someone can be asset-rich and cash-poor, wealthy on paper and unable to cover an emergency. The balance sheet tells you how much; liquidity tells you how reachable.

2.2 Personal Income Statement

Where the balance sheet is a photograph, the income statement is footage: it records what flowed in and what flowed out across a stretch of time — a month, a year. Subtract one from the other and you get the number that says whether the tank is filling or draining.

Definition 2.2 — Cash Flow

$\text{Income} - \text{Expenses}$, over a period.

The two documents are linked by a hose. Positive cash flow this month is water flowing into the tank; over a year, that flow is what raises the level the balance sheet measures. That is the precise sense in which you watch cash flow month-to-month and net worth year-to-year: the flow is fast and noisy, the level is slow and cumulative, and it is the flow, sustained, that eventually moves the level.

5. Common Confusions

Common Confusion

A raise doesn't help if expenses rise to match (lifestyle creep) — cash flow stays flat.

Note what this confusion is actually about: it is a statement about the income statement, not the balance sheet. A raise changes the top line of the video; lifestyle creep changes the bottom line by the same amount; the snapshot never moves. Reading only one of the two documents is what lets the mistake hide.

7. Interview Translation

“Walk me through the three financial statements and how they link” is close to a rite of passage in corporate-finance interviews. You've just built the personal version of two of them; the vocabulary transfers directly.

Interview Translation

If asked to distinguish the balance sheet from the income statement, lead with time, not contents:

“The balance sheet is a point-in-time snapshot — assets, liabilities and the equity residual on one date. The income statement covers a period — revenue minus expenses, ending in profit. They're linked: the period's retained profit flows into equity on the balance sheet, the way a month of positive cash flow raises net worth. So one is a stock measured at an instant and the other is a flow measured over an interval.”

The trap they're probing for: confusing profit with cash flow. The disciplined line is that a company — or a person — can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash, because timing and non-cash items sit between the two. Personally, the mirror is lifestyle creep: your income line can rise while your cash flow stays flat, because spending rose to meet it. Naming that a raise which lifts expenses one-for-one leaves cash flow unchanged tells an interviewer you read the whole statement, not just the top line — and it sets up the free-cash-flow discipline of Module 7.4.
