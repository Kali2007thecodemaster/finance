2. Reading a Stock Quote

A quote is a car's dashboard. No single dial is the car — speed, fuel, revs and temperature only mean something together. A price with no market cap is a speedometer with no idea how big the vehicle is. Here is an illustrative quote for a gold miner (a made-up ticker, real-shaped numbers, matching Module 7's running example). Click each field.

Read together, this dashboard tells a specific story: a \$9.6B mid-cap miner having a strong day, trading near the top of its yearly range on above-average volume, priced at 18× earnings, paying a token dividend, and — with a beta of 1.8 — expected to swing nearly twice as hard as the market. None of those facts is decisive alone; the skill is reading them as one picture, then asking whether the four pillars justify it.

3. Reading a Price Chart

Most serious charts are drawn as candlesticks. Each candle packs four numbers for one period — where price opened, closed, and the highest and lowest it reached. The shape tells you the story of the fight between buyers and sellers. Click the parts.

One candle is a period; a chart is a row of them, and the row is read for context, not for prophecy. Two dials matter as much as the candles themselves — the time axis (a day of candles and a decade of candles tell different stories) and the volume bars underneath (a big move on heavy volume is a firmer signal than the same move on a quiet day). Below is a real reading example: the “growth of \$10,000” line from the ZEQT fact sheet you're about to dissect.

Real ZEQT figures: a \$10,000 investment near inception was worth \$18,856 on Jul 31, 2026. The line's shape — up, a dip, then up harder — is exactly the volatility Module 06 taught you to sit through.

5. Common Confusions

Common Confusion

A high number of nominal holdings does not equal real diversification. ZEQT lists only 6 securities — yet each is an index ETF holding hundreds of companies, so the true count is enormous. Conversely, a thematic fund can list 100 holdings that are all one bet (Module 7.6's PAVE). Always look through to what the holdings actually are.

Common Confusion

Distribution yield is not total return. ZEQT's 1.28% yield is the cash paid out; the fund's growth (its 15%+ since inception) is a separate, larger thing. Chasing a high distribution yield while ignoring total return is how people end up with an income stream that slowly eats its own principal.

6. Interview Translation

“Here's a quote / a fact sheet — what do you see?” is a live-fire interview exercise. They're testing whether you read the whole instrument, and whether you know what each number does and doesn't tell you.

Interview Translation

Handed a quote, narrate it as one picture, not a list:

“Mid-cap name around \$9-10B, trading near its 52-week high on above-average volume, so there's real conviction behind today's move. It's at 18× earnings with a high beta, which tells me it's a cyclical, leveraged play — I'd want the macro and industry pillars to support that multiple before touching it. The token dividend says this is a growth/price story, not an income one.”

Handed the ETF sheet, lead with cost, structure and look-through:

“All-in-one equity fund, 0.18% MER, a fund-of-funds holding six index ETFs — so six line items but thousands of underlying companies, which is genuine structural diversification. Roughly 48% US, 25% Canada, the rest developed and emerging international; tech and financials heavy, medium risk. Short track record given the 2022 inception, so I'd weight the since-inception number lightly. As a portfolio core it's textbook.”

The tells an interviewer is listening for: reading market cap before price, checking volume to weight a move, quoting the MER unprompted, and — the senior move — looking through a holdings count to what's actually inside. That last one is the whole of Module 7.6 in a sentence.
